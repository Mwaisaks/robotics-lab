## Tagline (max 140 characters)
Pinch parts into the right bins on your real table: a hands-only mixed-reality puzzle game and a first step toward robotics practice.

## Inspiration (draft, about 120 words)
We are three trainers from Tukuze Afrika: Phenny teaches programming, and Mike and Joshua teach robotics. Through a program with CSA Africa, we work with secondary school students in Meru County, Kenya, at Ngine Secondary in Kaithe and Burieruri in Maua. The program exists so students get early exposure to technology and build their own solutions instead of depending on foreign ones. But robotics is hands-on, and equipment is limited. We wondered: what if a student could practice robotics thinking (reach, limits, placing parts correctly) with nothing but their hands, on their own table? That became Robot Arm Lab, a mixed-reality tabletop game where you pinch parts and drop them into the right bins while a robot arm reaches for each one. It's a first step toward robotics practice for learners.

## What it does (draft)
Robot Arm Lab turns a real table into a small robotics lab in mixed reality, using hands only (point and pinch). A level menu offers three levels:
1. **Sort the parts:** pinch parts and drop them in matching bins against a 60-second timer while a friendly robot arm follows your hand.
2. **Program the robot:** build a short program from blocks (forward, back, turn, repeat) and run it to drive a robot to a star, over three challenges.
3. **Light it up:** wire a battery, switch, resistor and LED into a closed loop on a simplified circuit bench, then build a small robot from wheels, motors and a controller board and watch it drive (a simplified demo).
A tick on the menu marks each completed level.

## What's next (draft)
Robot Arm Lab is a first step. Next we want to take learners further into robotics: deeper electronics and robot-building levels than today's simplified versions, and an advanced lab for joint control and simple control loops. See `docs/roadmap.md` (planned, not built yet). Any prize money would go toward inspiring young African robotics champions.
